# social
As a Mentor, you are "a sincere parent who values kindness and good manners and can nurture a caring heart."
## 1
You value not only your child's abilities and achievements, but what kind of person they grow up to be.
Doing well in school or sports matters, but you don't believe that alone makes parenting a success. You believe that greeting people, keeping promises and being able to see things from others' point of view are also great strengths in life.
Someday your child will leave home and live while interacting with many people. What they'll need then is not only knowledge and ability.
No matter how capable someone is, if others don't trust them, there's a limit to what they can do alone. On the other hand, people who value those around them find others who will help them in hard times and friends who will achieve things together with them.
That's why you also value small, everyday behavior.
Rather than special education, you teach how to relate to society through everyday conversation and the way you treat people at home. Valuing the growth of character, not just ability: that is the Mentor.
## 2
Children tend to grow up considerate of those around them and easily trusted by others.
Instead of acting only on their own feelings, they develop the habit of thinking about how the other person might feel. So in friendships and group life, they become able to act in cooperation with others.
In a family that values manners and promises, children also naturally learn the basic behavior needed in society.
These abilities aren't visible like test scores. But when children grow up and go out into society, they matter greatly as the power to be trusted by others.
On the other hand, children who care about others may put their own feelings last.
Being unable to say no to things they dislike. Pushing themselves too hard to meet others' expectations. To prevent this, you need to tell them that it's okay to value their own feelings just as much as caring for others.
Becoming a kind person is different from always holding yourself back.
## 3
What you should watch out for is not treating good manners and obedience as the same thing.
Greeting people. Keeping promises. Not hurting others. There certainly are things worth valuing in society.
But fitting in with others is not always right.
Sometimes they'll question school rules, or be unconvinced by an adult's opinion. They may also think differently from their friends.
If you keep demanding only that they fit in at such times, children may start choosing actions that won't get them scolded rather than thinking about what's right.
What you truly want to raise is surely not a child who simply does what they're told.
Why shouldn't they do that? Why should they value other people? If they can think that far, they can make their own judgments even where their parents can't see.
Valuing others and having your own opinions can go together.
Nurturing both at the same time is what matters for the Mentor.
## 4
You and your child are most likely to clash when you're concerned about how their behavior looks to others.
Things you'd allow at home may make you want to correct them at school or in public. As a parent, you want them to learn behavior that will serve them well in society so they won't struggle later.
But your child may not see your intention.
Why do I have to worry about what others think? What's wrong if I'm not bothering anyone? They may have questions like these.
If you end it there with "That's just how the world works," it may look to them as if you only care about what other people think.
What matters is thinking together about what the rule is for.
Is it to avoid hurting people? To help a group get along comfortably? Or is it something that has simply continued from the past and whose reason has faded?
By showing that you yourself think about it, your child also becomes able to consider the meaning of social rules instead of just accepting them.
Raise a person who thinks about why rules are kept, rather than one who simply keeps them.
When you can convey that much, your values will also reach your child more easily.
## 5
What suits you is an environment where your child has connections with people outside the home too.
It's good for them to interact not only with other children, but with people of different ages and positions. The sense of distance and consideration that's hard to learn at home alone develops little by little through relationships with people.
It's also important that the home itself is a place to learn how to value people.
If parents demand manners from their child while treating shop staff or family members rudely, their words carry no weight. On the other hand, the sight of parents naturally saying thank you, or apologizing when they're wrong, becomes a lesson for the child just as it is.
How to treat people isn't taught by explanation alone.
The adults children see at home every day are their first lesson in society.
And when your child makes a mistake outside the home, rather than just scolding, think together about how the other person felt. These accumulated moments lead to a kindness that is more than just a formality.
## 6
A great strength of yours is that you don't judge your child's life by ability alone.
Grades and schools are easy to express in numbers. But being able to value people and being trusted don't easily become numbers.
Even so, you understand that such strengths matter in life.
Being able to listen. Keeping promises. Being able to apologize when you were wrong. These small behaviors make a bigger and bigger difference as children grow.
And relationships touch every part of a child's life.
Whether at school or at work, no one can live entirely alone. Among people of equal ability, those others want to work hard with will be blessed with more opportunities.
You are not teaching your child manners just to be valued by society.
Being able to lay the foundation for living while building good relationships with others.
That is the great strength of the Mentor.
## 7
The flip side of your strength is that, in asking your child to be considerate of others, you may put their own feelings last.
Being able to care for others is a wonderful strength. But always giving way is not kindness.
Going along with friends even when you don't want to. Staying silent even when you doubt what a teacher says. Holding back alone so as not to hurt anyone. If this continues, it becomes self-sacrifice rather than consideration.
Sometimes saying no is necessary. Speaking up about something you don't agree with doesn't necessarily damage a relationship.
In fact, being able to express your own feelings while also listening to others is what truly gives a person the strength to live in society.
What you want to teach is not how to be seen as convenient by others.
It's how to respect others while respecting yourself in the same way.
Is your child holding back precisely because they're kind? Checking on that from time to time will help your strength be passed on in a healthier form.

Having both the strength to live in society and the strength to live as yourself.
Combining the two makes it easier to raise someone who is kind and can still speak their mind.

As words of Jesus Christ, the teaching "Love your neighbor as yourself" is well known.
What matters here is not just "love your neighbor." It also includes the part "as yourself."
You don't have to keep sacrificing yourself in order to value others.

Raise a child who can value people. And raise a child who, as one of those people, can value themselves too.

# security

At the root of this is a wish to make home a place where your child can feel safe.
In the outside world, there will be failures. There will be days when things don't go well with friends or when they lose confidence. If they kept being judged at home even then, they'd have nowhere to rest their hearts.
You want to be on their side regardless of results, not just accept them because they accomplished something.
No matter what happens outside, when they come home there's someone who accepts them.
The Guardian is someone who can give their child that kind of peace of mind.
## 2
Children tend to grow up able to express their feelings and ask others for help.
If they know they won't be harshly blamed when they fail, it becomes easier to tell their parents even about inconvenient things. They come to have the option of talking to someone instead of carrying problems alone.
The experience of being accepted at home also becomes a foundation for self-esteem.
With a sense that they have somewhere to come back to, they can take on the world outside home with confidence. In fact, a secure relationship with parents is what children need in order to grow away from them.
On the other hand, precisely because home is so comfortable, they may try to get away from hard things quickly.
When they hit something a little difficult, they may avoid even things they could overcome if they kept going.
What matters is not making them suffer, but helping them face difficulties while having a safe place to return to.
## 3
What you should watch out for is not treating protecting your child and removing difficulties as the same thing.
It's natural to want to help when your child is suffering. Especially since you value their feelings, you'll probably be careful about pushing them too hard.
But growing up sometimes requires a little hardship.
Practicing without improving much. Disagreeing with friends. Studying without getting the results they hoped for. Experiences like these can't all be avoided.
What matters then is neither making them quit immediately because they seem to be struggling, nor making them grit their teeth and carry on.
It's seeing what they're actually finding hard.
Are they struggling now but still want to continue? Or are they wearing themselves down to keep going? The way a parent should be involved changes depending on that difference.
A safe home is not a place where nothing hard happens. It's a place you can come back to even when hard things happen.
With that sense, your kindness becomes a strength that supports your child's challenges rather than stopping them.
## 4
You and your child are most likely to clash when your child is trying to keep going with something despite struggling.
Sometimes, even though they look clearly miserable to you, they say they don't want to quit. You'll want to tell them they don't have to push themselves.
But for a child, finding something hard and wanting to quit aren't always the same.
They may be frustrated at not getting to play in games, yet still love their club. Studying may be tough, but there may be a school they want to get into.
If parents keep creating escape routes ahead of time, the child may feel their parents don't believe they can overcome it.
On the other hand, there's no need to make them endure it "for their growth" when they've truly reached their limit.
What you need is not so much to judge their suffering, as to confirm together what they want to do.
If they want to continue, support them. If they want to step away, think together about a way that isn't running away. If they're unsure, don't rush to a conclusion.
Protecting doesn't always mean keeping them away from difficulty.
When they're trying to overcome something, believing in their strength and staying beside them is also an important role of the Guardian.
## 5
What suits you is a home where your child can fail without fear.
Not only being praised on days they bring home good results, but being able to come home as usual on days things went wrong. An environment like that brings out your strengths the most.
What's especially important is not making home too much a place of evaluation.
At school they're graded, in sports there's winning and losing, and among friends they worry about how they're seen. Outside the home, children are already exposed to plenty of evaluation.
That's why at home, they need time to be accepted for who they are, separate from whether they achieved something.
However, a safe place is different from a place where they don't have to do anything.
Because they could rest their hearts fully at home, they can go out again. Having failed and come back, they can try once more.
The home you make is not a place to hide your child from the outside world.
It's a place they can come back to even after going out, giving it their all and failing.
## 6
A great strength of yours is that results don't change your relationship with your child.
Children fail many times as they grow. Sometimes they can't achieve what their parents hoped for, and sometimes they lose confidence themselves.
At times like that, what they need isn't just more people evaluating them.
Knowing there's someone who accepts them even when they didn't succeed gives them the strength to stand up again.
When your child is down, you never lower their worth along with them.

## 7

Protecting your child is not the same as removing hardship from their life.

## 7
