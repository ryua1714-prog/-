# education
As an Architect, you are "a devoted parent, full of love and gifted at thinking about your child's future."
## 1
You are a parent who thinks not only about your child's present, but about what lies beyond it.
Rather than judging only by whether your child wants to do something or seems to be enjoying it right now, you think about where the experience might lead and what possibilities it will leave open. When it comes to schools, extracurricular activities, entrance exams and future paths, you don't leave everything to your child; you gather information yourself and try to find options your child doesn't even know about yet.
For you, education is not simply about getting good grades. It is closer to expanding future options by gaining knowledge and skills. That is why you sometimes recommend things even when your child isn't keen right now, if you believe they will be needed later.
At the root of this is a feeling that you never want your child's possibilities to shrink because you, as a parent, did nothing. Looking a little ahead of your child and shaping an environment that connects to their future: that is the Architect's way of parenting.
## 2
In Education Type families, children tend to grow up able to do what needs to be done.
Through homework, deadlines and tackling things they find difficult, they naturally learn that it's not enough to do only what they want—making their wishes come true also takes preparation.
And because their parents actively gather information, they come into contact with schools, activities, jobs and experiences they could never have found on their own. Among them, they may discover strengths they didn't know they had.
On the other hand, the more good options parents prepare, the fewer chances children have to choose from scratch. Children who keep receiving good suggestions from their parents about schools and activities may later find themselves wondering, "What do I actually want to do?"
That's why, for the Education Type, accepting experiences that stray from the prepared path matters just as much as preparing a good path.
## 3
The thing to watch most is whether your feeling of "this is for my child's sake" has grown bigger than your child's own feelings.
Most of what Education Type parents recommend comes from love. Studying increases your options, basic academic skills are worth having, and learning not to give up easily matters. Because these judgments are often actually correct, it can be hard to notice when you're going too far.
When your child says they want to quit an activity they've done for years, when they choose a different school from the one you thought best, when they become absorbed in something that seems useless for the future—in those moments, try separating two questions: will this choice really cause problems, or am I just feeling anxious?
You have the power to see the future. That's exactly why it's important, from time to time, to look away from the future and spend time looking at your child as they are now.
## 4
Education Type parents tend to clash with their children most when the child's thinking seems irrational to them.
Say your child suddenly announces they want to quit cram school. Your mind fills with what comes next: what will happen to their grades if they quit now, will they still be ready for entrance exams, could they keep going at a different school?
Meanwhile, what your child may be looking at is the present: "I don't want to go there tomorrow." In cases like this, neither of you is wrong; you are simply looking at different time frames.
The same thing happens with future paths. You think about life after attending that school, while your child thinks about wanting to attend it. You wonder whether that job can support a living, while your child thinks about wanting to try that job.
The more right you are, the harder it becomes for your child to argue back. But if this goes on, your child may gradually feel that you'll never understand anyway, and stop coming to you for advice altogether.
In those moments, before reaching a conclusion, try asking once: "What made you think that?" What matters is the attitude of trying to understand your child's thinking.
For a child, there is a big difference between being opposed before being understood and being opposed after being understood.
## 5
What suits you is an environment with plenty of information that still leaves room for choice.
When choosing schools, cram schools or activities, you'll work best in an environment with several options, where you can compare their features and also consider your child's personality and interests, rather than one where a single answer is presented.
On the other hand, being told to simply let your child do as they like, without enough information, may make you anxious. That's because you don't want to control your child; you want to choose with solid material in hand.
Gathering information is one of your great strengths. On top of that, leave a little room at the end: the parent widens the options, and the child chooses from among them—or even from outside them. In an environment like that, it becomes easier to balance your strengths with your child's independence.
## 6
Your greatest strength is that you can think about possibilities your child can't see yet.
A child's world is inevitably limited to what they already know. They can't aim for a school they've never heard of, or dream of a job they don't know exists. At times like that, you can gather information, find options and show your child a new world.
Another strength is that you don't judge only by the emotion of the moment. Even when your child says they hate something, you can think flexibly—what exactly they dislike, whether there's a reason to continue, whether there's another way—without clinging to a single method.
Being able to think about a future your child can't yet see: that is your strength as an Architect.
## 7
Your weakness is that you can say the right thing.
Studying increases future options, basic academic skills are worth having, learning not to give up halfway matters. In most cases, your views are well founded and are usually sound arguments.
That's exactly why, when you disagree with your child, you may find yourself trying to make them do as you say simply because you have more experience and more information.
But what you want to raise through education is surely not a child who can faithfully carry out what their parents say, but a person who thinks for themselves, chooses for themselves and walks their own path in life.
Sometimes it's important to accept that your child having ideas different from yours is itself one of the fruits of education.
Another thing to watch is that your attention easily goes to growth you can confirm with numbers. Grades, test scores, qualifications, rankings and acceptances are results that make growth easy to see. But growth such as learning to express their own opinion or finding something they love has no numbers.
Growth that can't be written into the blueprint is still certainly happening inside your child.
## 8
You are especially compatible with the Independence Type, The Counselor 🐴.
The Education Type finds options and widens the child's world, and the Independence Type then helps the child think about what they themselves want. When these two come together, it becomes easier for parents to set a direction while the child still decides their own life.
It's also a good idea to take in the perspective of the Individuality Type, The Producer 🐱.
While the Education Type thinks, "What should this child learn?", the Individuality Type thinks, "What is this child drawn to?" Precisely because it's a different viewpoint from yours, it can help you discover new sides of your child.
## 9

A Chinese thinker who placed great value on learning. His idea that people grow as human beings not just by gaining knowledge but by continuing to learn overlaps with the values of the Education Type.
@ Nobita's Mom (Doraemon)
A mother memorable for constantly reminding Nobita about homework, studying and daily habits. Her wish that he learn what he needs so he won't struggle later, even if he hates it now, makes her an easy image of an Education Type parent's heart.
## 10
Confucius's Analects contains the famous line, "Is it not a pleasure to learn and to practice what you have learned at due times?" It expresses the idea that there is value in repeating what you've learned until it becomes your own.
You, too, are probably someone who wants your child to learn and master many things now, so they can live on their own strength in the future.
Nobita's mom also keeps urging Nobita to do his homework and study. In her, we can see the very Education Type parental love that wants him to learn what he needs, so he won't struggle later even if he hates it now.
However, the goal of education is not to make your child walk the right path their parents have worked out. Let them learn many things, show them many worlds and help them build the strength they need. Beyond that, it also matters that one day your child can say, "This is the way I want to go."
Even if that path is different from what you imagined, you can accept that your child has grown to the point of thinking and choosing for themselves. That, too, is a great result of the education you have built up.
You are good at thinking about your child's future. So in the end, rather than completing the picture of the future you drew, let your child draw what comes next.

# individuality
As a Producer, you are "a big-hearted parent who values your child's uniqueness and can watch over their growth."
## 1
Rather than shaping your child toward a finished image of "the kind of person I want to raise," you want to find the individuality and talents already inside them and help those grow.
From studies and sports to personality and future careers, the world is full of standards about how things "should" be. But you don't believe that fitting your child to those standards is necessarily best.
Rather than spending lots of time raising weaknesses to average, you want your child to lose themselves in what they love and are good at, and grow it into a strength only they have. Even if they're a little different from those around them, as long as they seem happy, you can accept it as "part of who they are."
That's why you tend to notice changes that grades and rankings can't show. Loving to draw, being good at making people laugh, not being able to stop once they start looking something up. You can find value in small traits like these.
What you value most in parenting may be "raising a child to become someone only they can be."
## 2
Children tend to grow up able to express their likes, dislikes, strengths and weaknesses, with their own values and their own world.
They don't fear being different more than necessary, and find it easier to think "What do I want to do?" rather than "Everyone else is doing it."
In particular, you can expect a lot from how they grow once they find something they love. They dive deep into whatever interests them, and it may become one of their great strengths.
Having their individuality accepted by their parents also builds self-esteem. Even if they aren't recognized outside, they are understood at home. That experience also leads to the ability, as adults, to accept people whose values differ from their own.
On the other hand, precisely because they grow up with their interests respected, the ability to face "things I don't like but need to do" has to be nurtured consciously.
Developing strengths and running away from weaknesses are not the same thing. Once you can tell them apart, the strengths of the Individuality Type shine even more.
## 3
What you should watch most is believing you understand your child's individuality and, as a result, locking it in place.
"This child is more about sports than studies." "They're not the type to be in the spotlight." "They seem like a science kid." "This would suit them better."
These words come from watching your child closely, but your child is still growing. Something they were bad at when they were ten may be a strength by fifteen.
Something they started reluctantly can become something they love the moment they get good at it.
So when your child resists something, instead of immediately deciding "this isn't right for them," take a moment to consider whether they dislike it, or just aren't enjoying it because they can't do it yet.
Valuing individuality does not mean freezing their current individuality.
It's important to accept who your child is now while leaving room for new sides of them to appear—sides even they don't know about yet.
## 4
Because you basically respect your child's wishes, head-on clashes may not happen very often.
However, when your child starts wanting to be "normal," unexpected misunderstandings can arise.
To you, the individuality only they have is charming. But for a child, there can be times when being different from everyone else is painful.
Words like "You're fine just as you are" can, to the child, feel like "You don't understand how hard this is for me."
Conversely, when your child tries to escape from something they're bad at, the call also becomes difficult. Do you think, "It doesn't suit them, so it's fine to quit," or "This is the time to push them a little"?
In those moments, before deciding the answer for them, try asking, "Do you want to quit because you dislike it? Or because you can't do it well yet?"
What the Individuality Type needs is not to let the child do anything they like. It's being able to tell the moments to protect who they are from the moments to give them a push to expand their possibilities.
## 5
What suits you is an environment where your child can encounter many different worlds and find what they love among them.
Not only studies, but sports, art, music, making things, reading, nature experiences and interacting with people—an environment where they can touch as many different kinds of experiences as possible.
That said, there's no need to leave everything up to your child from the start just because "they should choose what they like."
Children can only choose from what they already know.
They can't fall in love with a sport they've never heard of, or dream of a job they don't know exists. Creating doorways into new worlds is something parents can do that matters greatly.
Parents widen the possibilities. What to choose is left to the child.
This division of roles suits the Producer very well.
## 6
A great strength of yours is that you don't judge your child by a single measuring stick.
At school, there are more and more moments of being judged by easy-to-read numbers like test scores and rankings. But not all of a person's charm or talent can be turned into numbers.
Being good at talking, making people laugh, being able to focus for hours, coming up with interesting ideas, being sensitive to others' feelings. Hidden in traits like these may be the great strengths that will support your child in the future.
You are the kind of person who can find them relatively early.
Even more important, you don't just find them—you affirm them.
Having someone who says "That's interesting!" or "Why not do more of it?" even before anyone else recognizes them. A single remark like that can turn a mere hobby into something that supports a child's whole life.
You can be the first person to understand a talent no one has recognized yet.
## 7
The flip side of your strength is that, in the name of "being true to who they are," you may also avoid the challenges needed for growth.
"They're bad at math, so maybe they're a humanities type." "If they're not interested in sports, they must be the artistic type." "They hate speaking in front of people, so they're an introvert."
Of course, that might really be the case. But whether it's individuality or simply a lack of experience isn't something you can tell right away.
Something they couldn't do becomes something they can do through practice. That experience itself can create a new side of your child's individuality.
Another thing to keep in mind is that strengths alone can't carry someone through all of life.
Studies they don't enjoy but need, promises to others, meeting deadlines, steady practice—even to express individuality, a minimum foundation is necessary.
That's why you should hold on to both "protecting who they are" and "believing they can do a little more."
## 8
You are especially compatible with the Strategy Type, The Strategist 🦊.
While you discover talents and individuality—"this child has this wonderful quality"—the Strategist is good at thinking about what kind of education and environment are needed to develop that quality.
The Producer finds the appeal of the raw material, and the Strategist builds a path from there. It's a very compatible combination.
Another type that tends to complement you is the Effort Type, The Coach 🐯.
The "loves" and "strengths" you discover take time to take shape as real talent. When the Coach's perspective of "believing in small, steady accumulation" is added, it becomes easier to grow a child's interest into a true strength rather than a passing phase.
You don't have to hold every way of thinking by yourself. By borrowing the perspectives of types different from yours, the strengths of the Individuality Type shine even more.

He placed no value on being the same as everyone else for its own sake, and searched for possibilities even outside existing frameworks. His way of life has much in common with the Individuality Type.

## 10
Sakamoto Ryoma is said to have said, "In this world there is never just one road. There are a hundred, a thousand, ten thousand roads."
There isn't a single right answer for your child's future either. It's not decided that if they're good at studies they take this road and if they're not they take that one, and there's no need to walk the same road as everyone else. The road that suits your child may lie somewhere neither you nor they can see yet.
Koro-sensei, too, says, "There isn't just one kind of talent. Find the assassination that suits your talent."
What matters is not only learning to do what others can do, but finding what your child has and searching for the place where it can shine. You have the strength to look at your child as they are, without judging them only by other people's standards.

# independence
The Counselor is "a reassuring presence who respects your child's thinking while reaching out a hand when needed."
## 1
You believe that, in the end, a child's life is something the child decides for themselves.
Having lived longer, parents have more experience and knowledge. There are many situations where you could make better judgments than your child. Even so, you don't believe that a parent continually choosing the right answers makes for good parenting.
What you value is your child thinking for themselves. When they're unsure about something, rather than handing them an answer right away, you listen to their thoughts and look for the answer together.
Even if a choice looks like a detour to you, you may want to let them experience it as long as it can be undone. Not only the experience of choosing and succeeding, but also the experience of choosing wrongly and rethinking, becomes strength for living on their own someday.
Of course, you don't let them do whatever they want. You teach them what they don't know. You give advice when they're unsure. And if something is truly dangerous, you stop them as a parent.
For you, parenting is not about guiding your child's life forever. It is also about gradually handing over the lead in their life as they grow.
## 2
Children tend to grow up with their own opinions, able to make their own decisions.
From an early age, they have chances to think about what they want. So instead of only looking for answers their parents will like or that others consider correct, they find it easier to develop their own standards of judgment.
This isn't something gained only through big decisions like choosing a future path. Small everyday choices—what to wear, what to learn, how to spend the weekend—also become practice in finding meaning for themselves and moving their own life forward.
And because they decided for themselves, they also tend to develop the ability to accept the results. Even when things don't go well, rather than blaming someone else, they become able to think about what to do next.
On the other hand, the more used children are to thinking for themselves, the more they may try to solve even their worries alone.
What's truly needed in the future is not someone who can do everything alone. It's someone who thinks for themselves and can also borrow others' strength when needed.
If you teach them to consult others and ask for help as much as you teach them to decide, the strengths of the Independence Type shine even more.
## 3
What you should be most careful about is not confusing independence with neglect.
Letting your child decide is important, but it requires material to decide with. You can't choose something you don't know exists. If you don't understand the differences between options, you can't judge which suits you.
For example, when choosing a high school, a child may only know the school's name and its academic ranking. Only when parents research things like the school culture, where graduates go, club activities and commuting time together does it become possible for the child to compare in their own way.
It's important not to leave it to them simply because it's their life, but to leave it to them after creating a situation in which they're able to choose.
Also, when your child comes to you for advice, just urging them to think for themselves may not be enough. Sometimes they don't want you to decide; they want someone to help them sort out their thoughts.
Sharing your opinion as a parent doesn't hinder independence. Hand over that opinion as one more piece of material, and let your child think it through in the end.
Independence doesn't mean deciding alone. It means using the information and opinions you need, and making the final decision yourself.
## 4
Because you usually respect your child's wishes, clashes from pushing your views on them may be relatively rare.
What's harder is when your child can't decide on their own.
For example, facing a decision about their future, they may repeatedly ask your opinion about which to choose. You may think, "It's their life, so I want them to decide in the end."
But what your child wants may not be the decision itself.
Maybe they're afraid of making the wrong choice. Maybe they just want to know what you think. Maybe they lack confidence in their own ideas and want a little push.
If you keep handing the question back to them in that state, they may feel not that they've been given freedom, but that they have to decide all alone.
In moments like this, try sorting through the options together. Tell them how you would think about it as a parent. And still, leave the final vote to them.
What you're aiming for isn't a child who never relies on their parents.
A child who can come to their parents for advice, but who in the end can decide for themselves.
Supporting them all the way there is what matters for the Counselor.
## 5
What suits you is an environment where your child can choose for themselves and experience the results.
At home, too, gradually widen the range of things your child can decide as they grow. When they're young, let them choose from a few options. As they get older, have them come up with the options themselves.
It's also important to let them experience failures that can be recovered from.
Even the experience of finding that a method they thought up and chose didn't lead to results becomes material for making better choices next time.
At the same time, parents don't need to step all the way back.
In most cases, parents are better at gathering the information needed. With high-quality support, children can grow even more. Sometimes it's also important to share opinions from a different perspective, or to stop them if something is truly dangerous.
As a Counselor, you're suited to an environment where the information you need is easy to get and you can encounter a variety of ways of thinking.
## 6
A great strength of yours is that you can respect your child as a separate person from yourself.
Parents have experienced many things before their children. If there's something you regret, you'd rather your child not make the same mistake. If something worked for you, it's natural to want to recommend the same path.
But you can stop and pause right there.
You understand well that a life that was good for you is not necessarily a good life for your child.
The more experience children accumulate choosing for themselves, the more they can make their own judgments even when their parents aren't beside them.
What you're trying to raise is probably "a person who chooses their own life and lives it with those choices as their own."
## 7
The flip side of your strength is that, out of respect for your child's wishes, you may hold back even the knowledge and experience you have as a parent.
Handing the decision to your child is different from a parent not giving an opinion.
There are worlds your child doesn't know yet. Schools, jobs, relationships—it's only natural that they can't judge what they haven't experienced.
At times like that, telling them what you know as a parent is not interference. Your experience, their teachers' opinions, their friends' stories—all of this information becomes material for them to decide.
Letting them experience failure also needs boundaries.
What they can learn by failing is not the same as what a parent should stop. Failures that can be recovered from can be left to them. But for things that are hard to recover from, such as health, safety or large sums of money, parents need to step in.
The range you entrust to them and the range you protect are not fixed. As your child grows, that boundary gradually moves outward.
More than giving freedom, growing the ability to use freedom.
Keeping that in mind makes the Counselor's weaknesses much easier to compensate for.
## 8
You are especially compatible with the Education Type, The Architect 🦉.
While you try to respect your child's wishes and leave the final choice to them, the Architect is good at thinking about the future and preparing the education and options needed.
The Independence Type alone may let a child choose only within the world they already know. The Architect alone may prepare too many paths that the parent thinks are good.
The Architect widens the world, and the Independence Type lets the child choose from within it.
Combining the two makes it easier to build a relationship in which parents widen the possibilities and the child chooses their life.
You don't have to become an Architect yourself. Just asking from time to time, "Before respecting their choice, have I shown them enough options?" will make the Counselor's strengths shine even more.

# challenge
As a Cheerleader, you are "a parent who treasures your child's curiosity, believes in their potential and can give them a push."
## 1
You want to widen your child's possibilities through experience.
When your child becomes interested in something, rather than judging first whether it suits them, you'd like to let them try it once. You know there are many things you can't understand without actually experiencing them.
Even if it turns out to be less fun than expected, you don't consider it a waste. Finding out that they don't like something is also a valuable experience for getting to know themselves.
On the other hand, something started on a whim can lead to an unexpected talent. Without narrowing possibilities from the start, you increase your child's options by letting them touch many different worlds.
What you value is being able to take on challenges more than never failing.
Instead of always running ahead to keep them from falling, you support them so they can get back up even after they fall. The Cheerleader is someone who naturally chooses that way of being involved.
## 2
Children tend to grow up with little resistance to new things, able to take a step toward whatever interests them.
They don't fear new places or unfamiliar people more than necessary, and develop the strength to widen their own world. Because they feel safe knowing their parents won't reject them if they fail, they're less likely to freeze up out of fear of the outcome.
Through many experiences, they also come to understand their likes, dislikes, strengths and weaknesses from real feeling.
Children don't know themselves from the very start either. Sometimes they find they love something because they tried it, and sometimes they realize something they admired isn't really for them.
By piling up such experiences, they come to know a self they couldn't understand just by thinking.
On the other hand, in an environment of constantly encountering new things, they may move on to the next thing before going deep into any one. It's important to nurture not only the number of experiences, but also the ability to dig deep once they've found something they truly love.
## 3
What you should watch out for is making experience itself the goal.
The stronger your wish to widen your child's world, the more experiences you'll want to give them. But more experiences are not always better.
It's important to pay attention to what your child actually felt.
Was it fun? Do they want to do it again? How was it different from what they expected? Just reflecting a little after an experience helps it stay with them.
Also, when your child says they don't want to try something, there's no need to immediately think they're being timid. They may simply not be interested, or they may not be ready yet.
Giving a push is the Cheerleader's strength, but sometimes waiting until your child moves on their own is also a form of cheering.
Not only giving experiences, but staying with them until those experiences take root inside them.
When you can do that, the many experiences you've given will connect to your child's growth.
## 4
You and your child are most likely to clash when something looks like a good experience to you, but your child isn't keen.
"It's a great opportunity, so just give it a try—you can always quit if it doesn't fit." That may feel natural to you, but your child may not feel the same way.
For a cautious child especially, stepping into a new environment takes a lot of energy.
If you keep demanding that they take on the challenge at such times, even though you think you're pushing them forward, they may feel rushed.
What matters is looking at why they don't want to take it on.
If they're simply not interested, perhaps there's no need to force the experience. But if they're interested yet frozen by fear of failure, taking just the first step together might change things.
Precisely because you're a Cheerleader, you don't always have to push forward.
Rather than making them take on challenges, raise a child who can move when they want to take one on.
Being aware of that difference turns your drive into strength for your child rather than a burden.
## 5
What suits you is an environment where your child can come into contact with many different worlds.
Rather than limiting your child's world to school and home, it's good to have places like sports or cultural activities where they meet different values. More than what they choose there, it means a lot just to learn that worlds they didn't know exist.
But there's no need to fill up their schedule.
When they're strongly drawn to something, it's fine to have a period of spending time on one thing rather than adding new experiences. A time to experience widely and a time to dig deep into one thing can coexist.
An atmosphere that doesn't treat failure as overly bad also fits your way of thinking well.
When they try and it doesn't work out, they're not blamed but can think about what's next. In an environment like that, children can act without fearing only the results.
What you should prepare is not an environment where they can always succeed.
It's an environment where they can take on challenges with peace of mind.
## 6
A great strength of yours is that you don't judge your child's potential only by who they are now.
You don't close off possibilities early with thoughts like "they can't do it now, so it doesn't suit them" or "they have no talent."
Children's abilities change through experience. Even if they can't do anything when they first try, finding it interesting can spark absorption, and before long it becomes a great strength.
And more important than talent itself is the chance to encounter it.
No matter how much potential they have, if they don't know that world exists, they can't even become interested. Parents creating doorways into new worlds matters greatly.
You also tend to accept failure as part of experience. When children know they won't be judged only by results, they can take a step forward with peace of mind.
Being able to give the first spark to possibilities not yet visible.
That is the great strength of the Cheerleader.
## 7
The flip side of your strength is that your attention may lean too much toward giving the next experience.
New experiences offer easy-to-see stimulation. Going somewhere for the first time or starting a new activity easily gives parents, too, a sense that their child is growing.
On the other hand, time spent continuing one thing for a long while looks like it has little change.
But once your child has found something they truly love, they need time repeating the same thing rather than adding new experiences. Little by little, what they couldn't do becomes possible, and beyond that there's a kind of fun that only then becomes visible.
And when your child says they want to quit partway, it's not always right to move on to the next thing right away.
Is it really not a fit? Or are they just going through a period when things aren't going well? That needs to be thought through together.
What you need is not fewer challenges.
After creating many doorways, it's also protecting the time to deepen the precious things they found there.
## 8
You are especially compatible with the Effort Type, The Coach 🐯.
The Cheerleader is good at giving children many opportunities and creating that first step into new worlds. The Coach, on the other hand, values building up what they've found over a long time.
With the Challenge Type alone, experiences may keep spreading out, moving on before any one thing becomes real strength. With the Effort Type alone, the focus on building things up may reduce chances to step into new worlds.
Try first. Then, once you find something you love, keep at it.
Challenge discovers possibilities, and accumulation turns those possibilities into strength.
Combining the two will bring even better balance to your parenting.
## 9
@ Toyotomi Hideyoshi
Toyotomi Hideyoshi is known as a man who started from a low social standing and eventually unified the country.
He was not someone with a future path laid out from the start. Through many positions and roles, he kept widening the places where he could make his mark.
Without deciding his potential based only on where he stood, he moved whenever an opportunity came, and carved out new paths. That image overlaps with the Cheerleader, who seeks to widen possibilities through experience.
@ Professor Oak (Pokémon)
Professor Oak isn't someone who guides children down one path from the start either.
He hands over the first Pokémon and entrusts them with a Pokédex, giving them the chance to go out into the wide world. What they meet and experience after that is up to them.
Instead of teaching answers from the start, he creates a doorway for stepping out into the world.
First go out and see the world with your own eyes.
That attitude of wanting to give children such experiences clearly represents the Cheerleader.
## 10
Toyotomi Hideyoshi is said to have left the words, "If you think you'll lose, you lose. If you think you'll win, you win. Even if it turns out the other way, tell others you will win."
Rather than freezing up while thinking about whether you can do it, believe in your own potential first and move. That attitude overlaps with your way of thinking as someone who gives your child a push.
Children don't know from the start what they're suited for.
Some things only become their passion once they actually try them. They may meet a precious friend at a place they bravely joined. And sometimes something they admired turns out not to be for them.
Both are meaningful experiences.
And what Professor Oak gave Ash was not a finished answer either. He handed over a first Pokémon and an incomplete Pokédex, and sent him off into a world he didn't yet know.
He prepared three starters, let him choose for himself and sent him off on his journey.
The experiences differ depending on the Pokémon chosen, and there are as many stories as there are Pokémon caught.
Giving children that kind of opportunity is exactly what a Cheerleader does.
Parents have a similar role.
You can't decide your child's future from the start. But you can show them the doorway, letting them know that such worlds exist.
What matters is not to keep them running all the way beyond that.
Precisely because you're good at giving your child a push, sometimes you also need to stay beside them when they stop. If, after many experiences, they become absorbed in one thing, it's fine to stop adding new stimulation and protect their time to stay there.
Try first. Treasure what you felt there. And once you find something you love, this time, stick with it patiently.
The greatest thing you can give your child is not success itself.
It's the courage to feel it's okay to step into a world you don't know.
With that courage, someday, even without a parent pushing them, your child will be able to walk into new worlds on their own.
